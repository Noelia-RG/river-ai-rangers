River AI Rangers — Lesson 2
Follow the Pollution: Upstream vs Downstream
Age group: 11–16 (KS3/KS4)
Duration: 60 minutes
Subject links: Science, Geography, Citizenship
Materials needed: Printed worksheets, multi-site river data (CSV or displayed on board), browser access to river-ai-rangers.streamlit.app, projector or screen


Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

Compare water-quality measurements across multiple sites on the same river
Identify likely sources of pollution by analysing what changes between sites
Construct a causal argument linking land use to water quality data
Produce an evidence-based briefing addressed to a specific stakeholder


Teacher Background (read before teaching)
This lesson uses data from three sites on the same river, collected across three seasons (March, June, September 2025):

Site A — Upstream woodland: Reference site above any human land use. Consistently healthy across all indicators.
Site B — Town edge bridge: Downstream from residential area. Elevated nitrates and phosphates, reduced invertebrate diversity.
Site C — Farm outflow downstream: Below intensive arable farmland. Critically high nutrients, algae blooms, no invertebrates recorded.

Key patterns to draw out:

Nitrates rise sharply from A → B → C (2.1 → 18.7 → 24.3 mg/L in March). Site C is over 10× the healthy limit.
Phosphates follow the same pattern (0.04 → 0.41 → 0.68 mg/L). Site C is nearly 7× the healthy limit.
Dissolved oxygen drops as nutrients rise — the algae blooms consume oxygen.
Invertebrate diversity collapses downstream: 6+ species at Site A, 1–2 at Site B, none at Site C.
The seasonal data (June worst, slight improvement by September) suggests fertiliser application timing may be a factor.

Students do not need to know all of this going in — the data investigation should lead them there.


Lesson Structure
1. Hook (5 minutes)
Display the three site descriptions on the board without the data:

Site A: Upstream woodland, above any farms or houses
Site B: Town edge bridge, below a residential area
Site C: 200m below intensive arable farmland

Ask: "Which site do you predict will have the best water quality? Which will be worst? Why?"

Take predictions — don't confirm or deny. Tell students the data will answer this.

Ask students to complete Part 1 of their worksheet before touching the AI.


3. Worksheet investigation — Part 1 (10 minutes)
Students work individually to:

Identify which readings are inside or outside healthy ranges at each site
Rank the three sites from healthiest to least healthy
Write one sentence explaining what they think is happening between Site A and Site C


4. AI investigation (20 minutes)
Open the River AI Rangers assistant. Upload the full CSV (all three sites, all three seasons) so the AI has the complete picture.

Model the first question:

"We have three sites on the same river. Site A is upstream woodland. Site C is below farmland. Nitrates go from 2.1 mg/L at Site A to 24.3 mg/L at Site C. What does this pattern suggest about where the nitrates are coming from?"

Then let students investigate using their prompt cards and worksheet questions. Encourage them to:

Ask the AI to explain the link between high nutrients and low dissolved oxygen
Ask what the invertebrate data tells us that the chemical data doesn't
Challenge the AI: "Could there be any other explanation for this pattern?"
Compare the seasonal data: "Why might Site C be worse in June than in March?"

Key teaching point: The AI can identify patterns and explain mechanisms. It cannot tell us who is responsible or what should be done — that requires human judgement. Push students to notice where the AI's answers end and their own reasoning must begin.


5. Stakeholder briefing (10 minutes)
Tell students: "You now have evidence that something serious is happening between Site A and Site C. Three people need to know: the farmer, the local council, and the Environment Agency. Each cares about different things."

Assign groups a stakeholder:

Farmer: Wants to understand the impact of their land management, not be blamed unfairly
Local council: Responsible for planning and drainage, needs clear evidence
Environment Agency: Regulates water quality, needs data cited precisely

Each group writes a 3–4 sentence briefing for their stakeholder using at least two real numbers from the data.


6. Close (5 minutes)
Ask one group from each stakeholder to read their briefing.

Then ask the class: "Did all three briefings use the same data? Why did they sound different?"

Close with: "This is what environmental science in the real world looks like — the same data, different audiences, different kinds of action needed."


Differentiation
For lower attainment:
Provide a pre-completed comparison table. Give sentence starters for the stakeholder briefing. Pair with a stronger student for the AI investigation.

For higher attainment:
Ask students to analyse all three seasons, not just March. Challenge them to explain the seasonal variation in nutrient levels. Ask: "What additional data would you want to collect to strengthen your case?"


Assessment
Formative: Listen during the AI investigation — are students moving from pattern recognition to causal argument?
Summative: Collect the stakeholder briefing. Does it cite specific data correctly and identify a clear audience and ask?


After the lesson
Collect all stakeholder briefings. The best ones can be sent — with student consent — to the actual river group, council, or EA contact.
If your river group has multi-site data, swap in their real readings for an even more powerful investigation.
